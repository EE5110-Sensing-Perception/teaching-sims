By the end of this guide you should be able to:

  • model an accelerometer as a spring–mass–damper and write its equation of motion;
  • define natural frequency, damping ratio, quality factor and bandwidth, and compute them from \(m\), \(k\), \(c\);
  • read a frequency response and predict whether a vibration will be passed, amplified or attenuated;
  • explain how a differential comb capacitor turns a nanometre displacement into a signal;
  • explain the trade-off between sensitivity, bandwidth and noise, and where the noise floor comes from;
  • explain two physical origins of accelerometer bias, and why sampling can alias vibration.

1. Why this matters

The accelerometer in a phone, a drone flight controller or a robot IMU is a MEMS device (micro-electro-mechanical system): a tiny silicon mass, a few micrograms, suspended on springs and surrounded by electrodes. Every property you will meet on a datasheet, such as range, bandwidth, noise density, bias and vibration sensitivity, is set by that mechanical structure and its electronics. Understanding the structure explains why an accelerometer on a vibrating motor mount produces nonsense, why datasheets specify an internal low-pass filter, and why higher-grade sensors are physically larger.

3. Background

3.1 Newton's law in an accelerating box

Newton's second law, \(F=ma\), holds in an inertial (non-accelerating) frame. An accelerometer measures motion of the mass relative to its case, and the case is accelerating. Let the case have acceleration \(a_{\mathrm{ext}}\) and let \(x\) be the displacement of the mass relative to the case. The mass's inertial acceleration is \(a_{\mathrm{ext}}+\ddot x\), and the only forces on it are the spring and damper:

$$m(a_{\mathrm{ext}}+\ddot x) = -kx - c\dot x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = -m\,a_{\mathrm{ext}} .$$

So the case acceleration acts on the mass like an applied force \(-m\,a_{\mathrm{ext}}\). If you accelerate the chip to the right, the mass lags behind, to the left.

Key idea. An accelerometer is a spring scale. At steady state the spring force balances the "inertial force": \(kx=-m a_{\mathrm{ext}}\), so \(x=-a_{\mathrm{ext}}/\omega_0^2\). Measure \(x\), multiply by \(-\omega_0^2\), and you have the acceleration.

Guide 4 will refine what "acceleration" means here: gravity acts on the mass and the case equally, so the sensor cannot feel it, and the quantity actually measured is the specific force.

3.2 Second-order systems in one page

Dividing by \(m\) and substituting \(\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}\) and \(\zeta=c/(2m\omega_0)\) gives the standard form

$$\ddot x + 2\zeta\omega_0\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = -a_{\mathrm{ext}} .$$

This one equation describes a car suspension, a guitar string, an RLC circuit and a MEMS proof mass. Its behaviour depends on only two numbers:

  • \(\omega_0\) sets the time scale: the response time is roughly one period, \(1/f_0\).
  • \(\zeta\) sets the shape. Below 1, the response overshoots and rings with a decaying oscillation. At \(\zeta\approx0.7\) it reaches the new value quickly with only about 5 % overshoot. Above 1 it creeps up slowly without overshoot.

3.3 Frequency response

If the input is a sinusoid \(a_{\mathrm{ext}}=A\sin\omega t\), a linear system's output is a sinusoid at the same frequency, scaled and shifted. With the readout scaled so that the gain is 1 at DC, the gain is

$$|H(\omega)| = \frac{\omega_0^2}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2 + (2\zeta\omega_0\omega)^2}} .$$
  • Well below \(f_0\): \(|H|\approx1\). The sensor reports the true acceleration.
  • Near \(f_0\): \(|H|\approx 1/(2\zeta)=Q\). With light damping, vibration is amplified.
  • Well above \(f_0\): \(|H|\approx(f_0/f)^2\). The mass cannot follow and the vibration is attenuated, falling 100× per decade.
Decibels. A gain \(G\) in decibels is \(20\log_{10}G\). The "−3 dB point" is where \(G=1/\sqrt2=0.707\). Log–log plots of gain are called Bode magnitude plots.

4. From displacement to signal: the comb capacitor

The mass carries rows of fingers interleaved with fixed fingers anchored to the substrate, like two combs pushed together. Each moving finger sits between two fixed fingers, with a gap \(g_0\) on each side (a couple of µm).

When the mass moves by \(x\), one gap shrinks and the other grows:

$$C_1=\frac{N\varepsilon_0A}{g_0-x},\qquad C_2=\frac{N\varepsilon_0A}{g_0+x},\qquad \Delta C=C_1-C_2\approx 2C_0\frac{x}{g_0}\quad(x\ll g_0),$$

where \(N\) is the number of finger pairs and \(C_0=N\varepsilon_0A/g_0\) is the capacitance at rest. Each capacitance alone is a nonlinear, \(1/\text{gap}\) function of \(x\). Their difference is almost linear, and anything that changes both equally (temperature, supply voltage, parasitic capacitance) cancels.

A capacitance-to-voltage converter reads \(\Delta C\), and the result is scaled to acceleration and digitised. Many higher-grade sensors instead run closed loop: electrostatic forces push the mass back to the centre, and the force needed is the measurement. That keeps \(x\approx0\), which improves linearity and range.

5. Sensitivity, bandwidth and noise

5.1 The sensitivity–bandwidth trade

The static displacement per unit acceleration is \(x/a = 1/\omega_0^2\). A softer spring (smaller \(k\)) moves more per g, which is easier to detect above electronic noise. But it lowers \(\omega_0\), and the bandwidth scales with \(\omega_0\). You cannot have both: halving \(f_0\) quadruples the sensitivity.

5.2 The Brownian noise floor

Gas molecules hit the mass randomly. The same damping that dissipates energy also injects random forces; this is the fluctuation–dissipation theorem. The result is a white acceleration noise with density

$$a_n = \frac{\sqrt{4k_BTc}}{m} = \sqrt{\frac{4k_BT\,\omega_0}{m\,Q}}\qquad[\text{m/s}^2/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}],$$

where \(k_B=1.38\times10^{-23}\) J/K is Boltzmann's constant and \(T\) is the temperature in kelvin. For a given \(f_0\) and \(Q\), a heavier mass is quieter, as \(1/\sqrt m\). This is one reason tactical-grade MEMS accelerometers are physically larger, and why they are often packaged in vacuum (high \(Q\)) with closed-loop control supplying the damping.

Reading a noise density. A density of 100 µg/√Hz with a 100 Hz bandwidth gives an RMS noise of \(100\times\sqrt{100}=1000\) µg = 1 mg. Halving the bandwidth reduces the RMS noise by \(\sqrt2\), not 2.

5.3 Vibration, filtering and aliasing

Robots vibrate: motors, propellers and gearboxes produce strong vibration from tens of Hz to several kHz. Three things can happen to it:

  1. Below the bandwidth, it is measured faithfully. That is correct, but it is noise as far as attitude estimation is concerned (Guide 4 shows averaging removes it).
  2. Near a lightly damped resonance, it is amplified, and the sensor may even saturate.
  3. Above the sample rate's Nyquist frequency \(f_s/2\), and not removed by an analogue filter before sampling, it aliases. The samples look exactly like a lower-frequency signal at \(|f - k f_s|\) for the nearest integer \(k\).
Practical consequence. Configure the IMU's internal anti-alias (digital low-pass) filter, mount the IMU on vibration isolators, and never assume that a low output data rate means vibration has been "averaged away". It may have aliased into the band you care about.

6. Where bias comes from

A perfect sensor at rest in zero acceleration outputs zero. Real sensors do not. Two physically different mechanisms produce the same symptom:

MechanismWhat happensLooks like
Electronic offsetThe mass is centred and the gaps are equal, but the readout electronics add an offset.Constant bias
Mechanical rest offsetResidual fabrication or packaging stress leaves the mass off-centre at rest, so \(\Delta C\neq0\).Constant bias \(= -\omega_0^2 x_{\mathrm{offset}}\)

Both drift with temperature and with package stress (e.g. after soldering), which is why the bias changes each time the sensor is powered up and during a run. Guide 4 shows how to calibrate the part that is constant and Guide 7 how a filter estimates the part that is not.

7. Worked examples

Example 2.1 — Natural frequency and sensitivity

Problem. The demo's default proof mass is \(m=10\) µg \(=1.0\times10^{-8}\) kg on a suspension of \(k=2.0\) N/m. Find \(f_0\) and the displacement for a 1 g acceleration.

Solution.

  1. \(\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}=\sqrt{2.0/10^{-8}}=\sqrt{2\times10^8}=14\,142\) rad/s.
  2. \(f_0=\omega_0/2\pi=2251\) Hz.
  3. \(x = g/\omega_0^2 = 9.807/(2\times10^8)=4.90\times10^{-8}\) m \(=49\) nm.

Interpretation. 49 nm is roughly one thousandth of the width of a human hair. A 1 mg signal moves the mass by 0.05 nm, less than the diameter of an atom. MEMS accelerometers work by averaging capacitance over a large finger area.

Example 2.2 — Damping, Q and bandwidth

Problem. With \(\zeta=0.35\), find the damping coefficient \(c\), the quality factor, and whether the response has a resonant peak. The demo reports a bandwidth of 3.19 kHz. Why is that above \(f_0\)?

Solution.

  1. \(c=2\zeta m\omega_0=2(0.35)(10^{-8})(14\,142)=9.9\times10^{-5}\) N·s/m.
  2. \(Q=1/(2\zeta)=1.43\).
  3. A resonant peak exists when \(\zeta<1/\sqrt2=0.707\). Here \(\zeta=0.35\), so the gain rises above 1 before rolling off, and the −3 dB point is pushed beyond \(f_0\) (to \(1.42 f_0 = 3.19\) kHz).

Interpretation. Bandwidth by the −3 dB definition does not guarantee accurate measurement: between about 1 and 3 kHz this sensor over-reports vibration. With \(\zeta=0.707\) the bandwidth equals \(f_0\) and there is no peak (a "maximally flat" response).

Example 2.3 — How big is the capacitance change?

Problem. The demo's comb has \(N=8\) finger pairs, overlap area \(A=500\ \mu\text{m}^2\) and gap \(g_0=2\) µm. Find \(C_0\) and \(\Delta C\) for 1 g.

Solution.

  1. \(C_0=N\varepsilon_0A/g_0 = 8\times(8.854\times10^{-12})(500\times10^{-12})/(2\times10^{-6})=1.77\times10^{-14}\) F \(=17.7\) fF.
  2. \(\Delta C\approx2C_0x/g_0=2(17.7\ \text{fF})(49\ \text{nm})/(2000\ \text{nm})=0.87\) fF \(=870\) aF.

Interpretation. The full 1 g signal is under a femtofarad; a 1 mg signal is under an attofarad (\(10^{-18}\) F). Real devices use far more fingers than the demo, and the readout electronics sit on the same die to avoid parasitic capacitance swamping the signal.

Example 2.4 — The Brownian noise floor

Problem. Compute the Brownian noise density for the default sensor at \(T=300\) K. What happens if the mass is doubled while keeping \(f_0\) and \(\zeta\) the same?

Solution.

  1. \(4k_BTc=4(1.381\times10^{-23})(300)(9.9\times10^{-5})=1.64\times10^{-24}\).
  2. \(a_n=\sqrt{1.64\times10^{-24}}/10^{-8}=1.28\times10^{-4}\) m/s²/√Hz \(=13\) µg/√Hz.
  3. Doubling \(m\) (and \(k\), to keep \(f_0\)) at fixed \(\zeta\) doubles \(c\), so \(a_n\propto\sqrt{c}/m\propto\sqrt{2}/2\): the floor falls to \(13/\sqrt2=9.2\) µg/√Hz.

Interpretation. Consumer accelerometers are typically 100–300 µg/√Hz, dominated by electronic rather than Brownian noise. Brownian noise sets the ultimate floor that no electronics can beat.

Example 2.5 — Vibration above the bandwidth

Problem. A 6 kHz vibration of amplitude 8 m/s² reaches the default sensor (\(f_0=2251\) Hz, \(\zeta=0.35\)). What amplitude is reported?

Solution.

  1. Frequency ratio \(r=f/f_0=6000/2251=2.67\).
  2. \(|H|=1/\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2}=1/\sqrt{(-6.11)^2+(1.87)^2}=1/6.39=0.157\).
  3. Reported amplitude: \(0.157\times8=1.25\) m/s².

Interpretation. Attenuated, but not removed. If the output is sampled at 1 kHz without an anti-alias filter, those 1.25 m/s² alias to \(|6000-6\times1000|=0\) Hz: they appear as a constant offset whose size depends on the relative phase. Vibration can become bias.

Problem. Compare springs of 0.5 N/m and 8 N/m with the same 10 µg mass and \(\zeta=0.7\).

8. Common mistakes

  • Treating the −3 dB bandwidth as "the range where the sensor is accurate". With light damping, the response is far from 1 near \(f_0\).
  • Assuming a low output data rate filters vibration. Without an anti-alias filter, it aliases.
  • Comparing noise figures without the bandwidth: µg/√Hz is a density, not an RMS value.
  • Believing a centred-looking mass means zero bias. Electronics can add offset independently.

10. Practice problems

  1. A sensor has \(f_0=5\) kHz. What is the static displacement per g?
  2. For \(m=10\) µg and \(k=2\) N/m, what damping coefficient gives \(\zeta=0.707\)?
  3. A resonance has \(\zeta=0.1\). By what factor is vibration at \(f_0\) amplified?
  4. An IMU samples at 800 Hz with its anti-alias filter disabled. A propeller produces vibration at 770 Hz. At what frequency does it appear in the data?
  5. A datasheet gives 150 µg/√Hz noise and you configure a 50 Hz bandwidth. What RMS noise do you expect, in mg and in m/s²?
  6. Explain in one sentence each why (a) a softer spring is more sensitive, (b) a heavier mass is quieter.
  7. Why does the differential capacitance \(\Delta C\) cancel a temperature change that expands both gaps equally?

12. Answers to practice problems

  1. \(\omega_0=2\pi\times5000=31\,416\) rad/s; \(x=9.807/31\,416^2=9.9\) nm.
  2. \(c=2\zeta m\omega_0=2(0.707)(10^{-8})(14\,142)=2.0\times10^{-4}\) N·s/m.
  3. \(1/(2\zeta\sqrt{1-\zeta^2})\approx5.0\). (Exactly at \(f_0\) the gain is \(1/(2\zeta)=5\); the peak is marginally higher, just below \(f_0\).)
  4. \(|770-800|=30\) Hz. A 30 Hz oscillation in attitude would look like a real motion to a controller.
  5. \(150\sqrt{50}=1061\) µg ≈ 1.06 mg ≈ 0.0104 m/s² RMS.
  6. (a) The spring force \(kx\) must balance \(ma\); a smaller \(k\) needs a larger \(x\). (b) The random thermal force grows only as \(\sqrt m\) (via \(c\) at fixed \(\zeta,\omega_0\)) while the inertia grows as \(m\), so the acceleration noise falls as \(1/\sqrt m\).
  7. If both gaps change by the same amount, \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) change together and their difference stays (to first order) at the value set by \(x\). Only an opposite change of the gaps, i.e. a displacement of the mass, alters \(\Delta C\).
