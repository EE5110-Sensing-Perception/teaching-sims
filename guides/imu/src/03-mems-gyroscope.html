By the end of this guide you should be able to:

  • explain what the Coriolis effect is, and derive the Coriolis acceleration on a vibrating mass;
  • explain why a MEMS gyro vibrates at resonance and holds its amplitude constant;
  • explain amplitude modulation and synchronous demodulation, and why they recover the rate;
  • define quadrature error, and show how a demodulator phase error turns it into bias;
  • compare mode-matched and mode-split operation in terms of sensitivity, bandwidth and noise.

1. Why this matters

A spinning-wheel gyroscope holds its orientation in space. A MEMS gyroscope has no spinning parts: it is a vibrating mass a fraction of a millimetre across, and it senses rotation through a tiny sideways force. That force is so small that the whole design, from the resonant drive to the demodulation electronics, exists to extract it. The same design choices explain the gyro errors you will model in Guide 5: bias, bias drift with temperature, and the limited bandwidth of high-sensitivity devices.

3. Background: the Coriolis effect

3.1 An intuition

Stand at the centre of a merry-go-round turning anticlockwise and roll a ball straight outwards. To someone on the ground the ball travels in a straight line. To you, turning with the platform, the ball curves off to the right. No real force pushes it sideways; the curve appears because your frame is rotating under the ball. In the rotating frame, that sideways drift is described by the Coriolis acceleration.

3.2 The equation

For a body moving with velocity \(\mathbf v\) relative to a frame rotating at \(\boldsymbol\Omega\), Newton's law written in the rotating frame gains an apparent acceleration:

$$\mathbf a_{\mathrm{Coriolis}} = -2\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v .$$

(There is also a centrifugal term, \(-\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r)\), which is negligible at MEMS scales and rates.) Its key properties:

  • It is proportional to both \(\Omega\) and \(v\): no motion, no Coriolis. That is why the gyro's mass must be kept moving.
  • It is perpendicular to \(\mathbf v\). Velocity along x with rotation about z gives acceleration along y.
  • It follows the velocity instant by instant. If \(\mathbf v\) oscillates, the Coriolis force oscillates in phase with the velocity.

3.3 Why vibrate?

Continuous motion in one direction is impossible on a chip, so the mass is made to vibrate: \(x=A\sin\omega_dt\), with velocity \(\dot x=A\omega_d\cos\omega_dt\). With rotation \(\Omega\) about z, the Coriolis acceleration along y is

$$a_y = -2\Omega\dot x = -2\Omega A\omega_d\cos\omega_dt .$$

This is a sinusoid at the drive frequency whose amplitude is proportional to the rate. The rate information rides on a carrier, like an AM radio signal. The drive is run at its mechanical resonance so that a small drive force gives a large amplitude \(A\), and the AGC holds \(A\) constant so the scale factor stays fixed.

4. The sensing chain

4.1 Sense-mode dynamics

The mass (or an inner frame) can also move along y on its own springs. Its motion obeys a second-order equation like the accelerometer's, driven by the Coriolis term and by an unwanted coupling from the drive:

$$\ddot y + \frac{\omega_y}{Q}\dot y + \omega_y^2 y = -2\Omega\,\dot x\ -\ \frac{k_{xy}}{m}\,x .$$

The sense motion is therefore a carrier at \(\omega_d\), filtered by the sense mode's response \(H(\omega_d)=1/(\omega_y^2-\omega_d^2+j\omega_d\omega_y/Q)\). With Coriolis only, the mass traces an ellipse in the x–y plane: x and y are 90° apart.

4.2 Synchronous demodulation

We have a signal \(y(t) = Y(t)\cos(\omega_dt)\) whose slowly varying amplitude \(Y(t)\) is proportional to the rate. To recover \(Y\), multiply by a reference at the same frequency and phase:

$$y(t)\cdot2\cos(\omega_dt) = 2Y\cos^2(\omega_dt) = Y + Y\cos(2\omega_dt).$$

The product contains the wanted slow term \(Y\) plus a ripple at twice the carrier frequency. A low-pass filter removes the ripple, leaving \(Y\), the rate (after scaling by the known sensitivity).

Key idea. Demodulation is phase-sensitive. A signal exactly 90° from the reference averages to zero after multiplication, because \(\sin\cdot\cos\) averages to zero. That is how the gyro rejects quadrature error, and why a phase error in the reference is so damaging.

The reference is the drive velocity, delayed by the sense mode's phase \(\angle H(\omega_d)\) (which the manufacturer calibrates). Real gyros compute both the I channel (rate) and the Q channel (quadrature), and often use the Q channel to monitor or null the quadrature.

5. Quadrature error and bias

Etching is never perfect. If the springs are slightly skewed, driving the mass along x also pushes it a little along y, in proportion to the displacement \(x\). This is the \(k_{xy}x/m\) term. Expressed as the rate that would produce the same sense amplitude, it is

$$\Omega_q = \frac{k_{xy}}{2m\omega_d}.$$

\(\Omega_q\) is often hundreds of deg/s, far larger than the rates you want to measure. It is only rejected because it is 90° from the Coriolis signal. If the demodulator's reference has a phase error \(\varphi\), the output becomes

$$\hat\Omega = \Omega\cos\varphi - \Omega_q\sin\varphi .$$

The sense-mode phase depends on \(\omega_y\) and \(Q\), and both drift with temperature, so \(\varphi\) and hence the bias drift too. This is a root cause of turn-on bias and in-run bias instability (Guide 5). Manufacturers fight it with quadrature-nulling electrodes, phase calibration and temperature compensation.

6. Mode matching: sensitivity vs bandwidth

The sense amplitude per unit rate is set by \(|H(\omega_d)|\):

  • Mode split by \(\Delta\omega=\omega_y-\omega_d\): \(|H|\approx 1/(2\omega_d\Delta\omega)\). The sense mode is off resonance, so the sensitivity is modest, but the rate response is flat up to roughly \(\Delta f\): a fast sensor.
  • Mode matched (\(\omega_y=\omega_d\)): \(|H|=Q/\omega_d^2\). The sense mode is at resonance, so the motion is about \(Q\) times larger per deg/s, and the rate noise is lower. But a resonance with quality factor \(Q\) takes about \(Q\) cycles to build up, so the rate bandwidth shrinks to about \(f_d/(2Q)\).

Consumer MEMS gyros typically run split-mode for robustness and bandwidth. Navigation-grade MEMS push toward matched mode and recover bandwidth with closed-loop force rebalance: electrodes actively hold the sense motion at zero, and the force required becomes the output, with a bandwidth set by the control loop rather than by \(Q\).

7. Worked examples

Example 3.1 — How big is the Coriolis acceleration?

Problem. The demo drives a mass at \(f_d=5\) kHz with amplitude \(A=5\) µm. Find the peak drive velocity and the peak Coriolis acceleration for a 100 °/s rotation.

Solution.

  1. \(\omega_d=2\pi\times5000=31\,416\) rad/s.
  2. \(v_{\max}=A\omega_d=(5\times10^{-6})(31\,416)=0.157\) m/s.
  3. \(\Omega=100^\circ/\text{s}=1.745\) rad/s.
  4. \(a_{\max}=2\Omega v_{\max}=2(1.745)(0.157)=0.548\) m/s² \(\approx0.056\,g\).

Interpretation. A brisk 100 °/s turn produces only 0.056 g of Coriolis acceleration, and it oscillates at 5 kHz. The Earth's rotation (15 °/h \(=7.3\times10^{-5}\) rad/s) gives \(2.3\times10^{-5}\) m/s², about 2 µg. That is why consumer MEMS gyros cannot sense Earth rate.

Example 3.2 — Sense displacement, split vs matched

Problem. Using Example 3.1, find the sense amplitude for 100 °/s with (a) a 300 Hz mode split and (b) matched modes with \(Q=200\).

Solution.

  1. (a) \(|H|\approx1/|\omega_y^2-\omega_d^2|=1/\big((2\pi)^2(5300^2-5000^2)\big)=1/(39.48\times3.09\times10^6)=8.2\times10^{-9}\) s².
    \(y=0.548\times8.2\times10^{-9}=4.5\times10^{-9}\) m = 4.5 nm, i.e. 45 pm per °/s.
  2. (b) \(|H|=Q/\omega_d^2=200/(9.87\times10^8)=2.03\times10^{-7}\) s².
    \(y=0.548\times2.03\times10^{-7}=1.1\times10^{-7}\) m = 111 nm, i.e. 1.1 nm per °/s.

Interpretation. Matching the modes gives about 25× more motion per °/s, which is a big noise advantage (the demo reports 44.9 vs 1111 pm per °/s). The price is bandwidth: see Example 3.4.

Example 3.3 — Bias from a phase error

Problem. A gyro has 200 °/s-equivalent quadrature. The demodulator phase is off by 3°. What bias results? What phase accuracy is needed for a bias below 0.1 °/s?

Solution.

  1. Bias \(=-\Omega_q\sin\varphi=-200\sin3^\circ=-10.5\) °/s.
  2. For \(|{\rm bias}|<0.1\): \(\sin\varphi<0.1/200=5\times10^{-4}\), so \(\varphi<0.029^\circ\).

Interpretation. The phase must be held to a few hundredths of a degree across temperature. Reducing \(\Omega_q\) itself, by quadrature-nulling electrodes, relaxes that requirement in proportion.

Example 3.4 — Rate bandwidth

Problem. Estimate the rate bandwidth of the matched-mode gyro with \(Q=200\) and with \(Q=1000\). Could either measure a 40 Hz vibration of a drone frame?

Solution. \(f_{\mathrm{BW}}\approx f_d/(2Q)\):

  • \(Q=200\): \(5000/400=12.5\) Hz (the demo computes 12.5 Hz).
  • \(Q=1000\): \(5000/2000=2.5\) Hz.

Answer. Neither: a 40 Hz rate oscillation would be strongly attenuated. The 300 Hz split-mode device (bandwidth about 460 Hz before the output filter) tracks it easily.

Example 3.5 — Demodulation by hand

Problem. The sense signal is \(y=Y_c\cos\omega_dt + Y_q\sin\omega_dt\), with \(Y_c\) from Coriolis and \(Y_q\) from quadrature. Show what the I channel (reference \(2\cos(\omega_dt+\varphi)\)) outputs after low-pass filtering.

Solution.

  1. Use \(2\cos a\cos b=\cos(a-b)+\cos(a+b)\) and \(2\sin a\cos b=\sin(a-b)+\sin(a+b)\).
  2. \(2Y_c\cos\omega_dt\cos(\omega_dt+\varphi)=Y_c[\cos\varphi+\cos(2\omega_dt+\varphi)]\).
  3. \(2Y_q\sin\omega_dt\cos(\omega_dt+\varphi)=Y_q[-\sin\varphi+\sin(2\omega_dt+\varphi)]\).
  4. The low-pass filter removes the \(2\omega_d\) terms, leaving \(Y_c\cos\varphi - Y_q\sin\varphi\).

Answer. This is exactly \(\hat\Omega=\Omega\cos\varphi-\Omega_q\sin\varphi\) after scaling. With \(\varphi=0\) the quadrature vanishes completely.

8. Common mistakes

  • Thinking a MEMS gyro "holds orientation" like a spinning wheel. It measures rate; orientation comes only from integrating that rate (Guide 5).
  • Confusing the drive frequency (kHz, internal) with the rate bandwidth (Hz to hundreds of Hz, what you see at the output).
  • Treating bias as a random fixed number. Much of it has a physical cause that drifts with temperature.

12. Answers to practice problems

  1. \(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v=\hat z\times\hat x=\hat y\), so \(\mathbf a=-2\Omega v\,\hat y\): along −y.
  2. The Coriolis force scales with \(A\), so the sense amplitude falls by 10 % while the calibrated scale factor assumes the old amplitude: the reported rate is 45 °/s, a −10 % scale-factor error.
  3. \(\Delta b\approx\Omega_q\,\Delta\varphi=50\times0.2\times\pi/180=0.17\) °/s = 630 °/h, far above an industrial bias-instability spec, so compensation is essential.
  4. A resonator's amplitude builds up or decays with time constant \(2Q/\omega_d\), i.e. about \(Q/\pi\) cycles; the sense amplitude cannot follow a rate change faster than that.
  5. The rate information is at \(\omega_d\) (kHz), not near DC. A low-pass filter would remove the signal itself; the multiplication shifts it down to DC first.
  6. 1 °/h = 1/3600 °/s, so \(45\ \text{pm}/3600\approx0.012\) pm, about a thousandth of an atom's diameter. Such rates are only visible after averaging many cycles, and even then the noise dominates. Consumer MEMS gyros cannot resolve Earth rate for this reason.
