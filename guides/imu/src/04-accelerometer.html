By the end of this guide you should be able to:

  • state precisely what an accelerometer measures (specific force), and predict its reading at rest, in free fall and when accelerating;
  • derive roll and pitch from a static reading, and explain why yaw cannot be recovered;
  • explain why bias, sustained acceleration and lever-arm effects all masquerade as tilt, while vibration averages out;
  • write the deterministic error model (bias, scale factor, misalignment), and estimate it with a six-position calibration;
  • compute and compensate the lever-arm acceleration of an IMU mounted away from the rotation centre.

1. Why this matters

The accelerometer is the IMU's "which way is down" sensor. Every attitude filter uses it to correct gyro drift in roll and pitch, and every inertial navigator integrates it for velocity and position. Both uses depend on understanding exactly what it measures, because the quantity is not what the name suggests: a stationary accelerometer does not read zero, and a falling one does.

2. Key terms

TermDefinition
Specific force \(\mathbf f\)Non-gravitational force per unit mass acting on the sensor: \(\mathbf f=\mathbf a-\mathbf g\). This is what an accelerometer measures. Units m/s² (or g).
Gravity vector \(\mathbf g\)The gravitational acceleration, pointing down. In NED, \(\mathbf g^n=[0,0,+g]\), with \(g\approx9.81\) m/s².
Kinematic acceleration \(\mathbf a\)The second derivative of position relative to an inertial frame; what you would get by differentiating GPS position twice.
Quasi-staticMoving slowly enough that \(\mathbf a\approx\mathbf 0\), so \(\mathbf f\approx-\mathbf g\).
TiltRoll and pitch together: the orientation of the body relative to the horizontal.
ObservableA quantity that can be determined from the available measurements. Yaw is not observable from gravity.
Bias \(\mathbf b\)A constant (or slowly varying) offset added to the output.
Scale-factor error \(s\)A fractional gain error: the output is \((1+s)\) times the input. Quoted in % or ppm (parts per million).
Misalignment / non-orthogonalitySensor axes that are not exactly aligned with, or perpendicular to, the body axes, so each axis picks up a little of the others. Quoted in mrad.
Least squaresChoosing the parameters that minimise the sum of squared differences between model and data.
Lever arm \(\mathbf r\)The position of the IMU relative to the point whose motion you care about (often the centre of rotation).
Centripetal accelerationThe acceleration \(\omega^2r\), directed toward the axis, of a point moving in a circle of radius \(r\) at rate \(\omega\).

3. What an accelerometer measures

3.1 Gravity cannot be felt

The proof mass of Guide 2 and the case around it are both pulled by gravity with the same acceleration. So gravity alone produces no relative motion: a sensor in free fall reads zero, just as an astronaut in orbit feels weightless. What the sensor does respond to are the forces that act on the case but not directly on the mass: the table pushing up, the motor pushing forward, air drag. Their sum per unit mass is the specific force:

$$\mathbf f = \mathbf a - \mathbf g,\qquad \mathbf f^b = R^b_n\,(\mathbf a^n-\mathbf g^n).$$
Key idea. At rest and level in NED/FRD, an accelerometer reads \(\mathbf f^b=[0,\,0,\,-g]\): minus 9.81 on the z axis, because z points down and the reaction force points up. In ROS FLU the same sensor reads \([0,0,+g]\). If your level IMU reads \(+9.81\) on an axis you think points down, you have a sign or frame error.

3.2 Tilt from gravity

If the platform is quasi-static, \(\mathbf f^b\approx-R^b_n\mathbf g^n\): the accelerometer measures the direction of "up" in body axes. Substituting the ZYX rotation from Guide 1 (§5.2) with \(\mathbf g^n=[0,0,g]\):

$$\mathbf f^b = -g\begin{bmatrix}-\sin\theta\\ \sin\phi\cos\theta\\ \cos\phi\cos\theta\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}g\sin\theta\\ -g\sin\phi\cos\theta\\ -g\cos\phi\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}.$$

Solving for the angles:

$$\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(-f_y,\,-f_z),\qquad \theta=\operatorname{atan2}\!\Big(f_x,\ \sqrt{f_y^2+f_z^2}\Big).$$

Yaw is unobservable. Yaw \(\psi\) does not appear in \(\mathbf f^b\) at all. Rotating about the vertical leaves the direction of gravity unchanged, so no accelerometer reading can reveal it. Heading needs another reference: a magnetometer (Guide 6), GNSS, or vision.

3.3 Things that look like tilt

The tilt formulas assume the only specific force is the reaction to gravity. Anything else along x is interpreted as pitch. An extra \(d\) along x produces a pitch error of \(\arctan(d/g)\approx d/g\) radians:

SourceExtra specific forceApparent pitch error
Consumer bias, 10 mg0.1 m/s²0.58°
Car accelerating1 m/s²5.8° nose-up
IMU 0.5 m from the spin axis at 120 °/s2.19 m/s² toward the axis12.6°

A forward acceleration reads as nose-up. Pilots experience the same illusion (the somatogravic illusion): the inner ear is an accelerometer too.

3.4 Noise and vibration average out; bias does not

Zero-mean disturbances, such as white noise and vibration, shrink when averaged. For \(N\) independent samples with standard deviation \(\sigma\), the mean has standard deviation

$$\sigma_{\bar x}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt N}.$$

Averaging 100 samples reduces the noise 10×. A bias, a sustained acceleration or a steady lever-arm term is the same in every sample, so averaging does nothing to it.

This is why gravity is a good low-frequency reference for tilt: correct on average, unreliable instant by instant. Guide 7 builds the complementary filter on exactly this idea.

4. Deterministic error model

A real triad of accelerometers reports

$$\tilde{\mathbf f} = (I+S)\,M\,\mathbf f + \mathbf b + \mathbf n,$$
  • \(\mathbf b\), bias: a turn-on part that differs every power cycle, plus an in-run part that drifts (Guide 5 models it).
  • \(S=\operatorname{diag}(s_x,s_y,s_z)\), scale-factor errors: the gain of each axis is slightly wrong.
  • \(M\approx I+\) small off-diagonal terms, misalignment / non-orthogonality: axis x also sees a little of true y and z, and so on.
  • \(\mathbf n\), white noise.

The physical origins, from Guide 2: bias from electronic and mechanical offsets; scale from spring stiffness, gap and gain tolerances; misalignment from die placement, package and PCB mounting.

Key idea. A bias is the same whatever the input. Scale and misalignment errors are proportional to the input, so they can hide when the relevant input is zero. A level sensor has \(f_x=0\), so an x scale error is invisible; tilt it and the error appears. A misalignment that couples z into x is not hidden when level, because \(f_z=-g\) is large.

5. Six-position calibration

5.1 Least squares in one paragraph

Suppose a measurement \(y\) depends linearly on unknown parameters \(\mathbf p\): \(y_k=\mathbf h_k^\top\mathbf p+\text{noise}\). Stack \(K\) measurements into \(\mathbf y=H\mathbf p+\mathbf n\). If there are more equations than unknowns, choose \(\mathbf p\) to minimise \(\|\mathbf y-H\mathbf p\|^2\). The solution is

$$\hat{\mathbf p}=(H^\top H)^{-1}H^\top\mathbf y,$$

which numerical libraries compute stably (e.g. numpy.linalg.lstsq).

5.2 The procedure

  1. Place the IMU on a level surface in six static poses: each axis pointing up, then down. The true specific force in pose \(k\) is known: \(\mathbf f_k=\pm g\,\mathbf e_i\).
  2. Average many samples in each pose to suppress noise (\(\sigma/\sqrt N\)).
  3. For each sensor axis \(i\), write \(\bar y_{k,i}=\mathbf G_i^\top\mathbf f_k+b_i\), where \(\mathbf G_i\) is row \(i\) of \(G=(I+S)M\). That is 6 equations and 4 unknowns per axis (18 and 12 in total). Solve by least squares.
  4. Correct live data with \(\hat{\mathbf f}=\hat G^{-1}(\tilde{\mathbf f}-\hat{\mathbf b})\).

The test only needs gravity and a flat surface; a precision cube or a fixture makes the poses repeatable. Gyros are harder: a rest average gives the gyro bias, but gyro scale factors need a rate table.

6. The lever arm

Rigid-body kinematics says a point at offset \(\mathbf r\) from a reference point on a rotating body has acceleration

$$\mathbf a_{\mathrm{IMU}} = \mathbf a_{\mathrm{ref}} + \dot{\boldsymbol\omega}\times\mathbf r + \boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r).$$
  • \(\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)\) is the centripetal term, of magnitude \(\omega^2r\), pointing toward the rotation axis.
  • \(\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}\times\mathbf r\) is the tangential (Euler) term, present while the rate changes.

An IMU mounted off-centre therefore reports extra specific force whenever the robot turns, and an accelerometer-only tilt estimate "leans". Two fixes: mount the IMU near the rotation centre, or subtract the predicted terms using the gyro rate and a measured \(\mathbf r\) (differentiating the gyro for \(\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}\), which is noisy).

7. Worked examples

Example 4.1 — Tilt from a reading

Problem. A quasi-static IMU (NED/FRD) reads \(\mathbf f^b=[2.0,\ -1.5,\ -9.5]\) m/s². Find roll and pitch, and check the reading is plausible.

Solution.

  1. Roll: \(\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(-f_y,-f_z)=\operatorname{atan2}(1.5,\ 9.5)=8.97^\circ\).
  2. Pitch: \(\theta=\operatorname{atan2}\big(2.0,\ \sqrt{1.5^2+9.5^2}\big)=\operatorname{atan2}(2.0,\ 9.618)=11.75^\circ\).
  3. Check: \(\|\mathbf f\|=9.82\) m/s², within 0.2 % of \(g\). If \(\|\mathbf f\|\) were, say, 11 m/s², the platform would not be quasi-static and the angles would be unreliable.

Answer. Roll ≈ 9.0° (right wing down), pitch ≈ 11.7° (nose up).

Example 4.2 — Bias as tilt

Problem. A level robot's accelerometer has a 0.5 m/s² bias on x. What pitch does it report? How does that compare with a 2 m/s² forward acceleration?

Solution. Pitch error \(=\arctan(d/g)\):

  • Bias: \(\arctan(0.5/9.807)=2.92^\circ\).
  • Surge: \(\arctan(2/9.807)=11.5^\circ\) nose-up.

Interpretation. A single static reading cannot distinguish these from genuine tilts of 2.9° and 11.5°. Calibration removes the bias; only a model of the motion (Guide 7, Guide 8) can remove the surge.

Example 4.3 — How much does averaging help?

Problem. Accelerometer noise is 0.02 m/s² per sample at 100 Hz. What is the standard deviation of a pitch estimate from a 1 s average?

Solution.

  1. 100 samples: \(\sigma_{\bar f}=0.02/\sqrt{100}=0.002\) m/s².
  2. Pitch sensitivity near level: \(\delta\theta\approx\delta f_x/g\), so \(\sigma_\theta=0.002/9.807=2.0\times10^{-4}\) rad \(=0.012^\circ\).

Interpretation. Noise is not the problem for static tilt; bias is. A 0.02 m/s² bias would give 0.12°, ten times the averaged noise.

Example 4.4 — A one-axis calibration by hand

Problem. With the x axis pointing up, the averaged reading is \(+9.95\) m/s². Pointing down, it is \(-9.71\) m/s². Ignoring misalignment, find the x bias and scale-factor error.

Solution. The model is \(\tilde f=(1+s)f+b\), with \(f=+g\) up and \(-g\) down:

  1. Add the two readings: \(9.95+(-9.71)=2b\), so \(b=0.12\) m/s² (about 12 mg).
  2. Subtract: \(9.95-(-9.71)=2(1+s)g\), so \(1+s=19.66/19.613=1.0024\), i.e. \(s=0.24\,\%\).

Answer. Bias 0.12 m/s², scale error +0.24 %. The six-position method does the same for all three axes at once, and adds the misalignment terms by using the readings of the other axes in each pose.

Example 4.5 — The spinning robot

Problem. An IMU is mounted 0.5 m ahead of a robot's turning centre, and the robot spins in place at 120 °/s. What does an accelerometer-only tilt estimator report?

Solution.

  1. \(\omega=120^\circ/\text{s}=2.094\) rad/s.
  2. Centripetal magnitude: \(\omega^2r=2.094^2\times0.5=2.19\) m/s², pointing toward the axis, i.e. along −x.
  3. So \(f_x=-2.19\), and pitch \(=\arctan(-2.19/9.807)=-12.6^\circ\): nose-down.

Interpretation. The demo's "Lever arm: spinning robot" scenario shows exactly −12.6°. Compensation subtracts \(\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)\) using the gyro rate and the known \(\mathbf r\).

8. Common mistakes

  • Expecting a stationary accelerometer to read zero, or a level one to read \(+g\) on a down-pointing axis.
  • Estimating tilt from a single sample, or while the vehicle is accelerating.
  • Averaging to remove a bias. Averaging removes noise, not bias.
  • Calibrating in only one orientation, which cannot separate bias from scale and misalignment.
  • Ignoring the lever arm on robots that turn quickly (legged robots, spinning drones, arm end-effectors).

12. Answers to practice problems

  1. Upside down (roll 180°): \(\mathbf f^b=[0,0,+g]\). The support still pushes "up", which is now along body +z.
  2. \(\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(-4.905,\ 8.496)=-30^\circ\); \(\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(0,\ 9.807)=0^\circ\). Rolled 30° left wing down, level in pitch.
  3. \(\arctan(3/9.807)=17.0^\circ\), nose-up.
  4. \(\sqrt N=0.05/0.001=50\), so \(N=2500\) samples (25 s at 100 Hz).
  5. Up and down readings are \(b+(1+s)g\) and \(b-(1+s)g\): their sum isolates the bias and their difference the scale factor. With one orientation, bias and scale are confounded.
  6. \(\omega=1.047\) rad/s: centripetal \(\omega^2r=0.877\) m/s². \(\dot\omega=1.571\) rad/s²: tangential \(\dot\omega r=1.257\) m/s².
  7. Gravity only ever defines the vertical, and rotating the IMU about the vertical does not change the gravity components. Yaw misalignment needs a horizontal reference: a known motion (e.g. driving straight), a magnetometer or an external measurement.
