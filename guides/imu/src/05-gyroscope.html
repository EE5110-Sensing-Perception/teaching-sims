By the end of this guide you should be able to:

  • integrate gyro rate into angle, and predict how each error term grows with time;
  • explain white noise, random walk, bias instability and rate random walk, with their units;
  • convert between datasheet units and simulation parameters without unit mistakes;
  • compute an Allan deviation, and read ARW, bias instability and rate random walk from its slopes;
  • choose an IMU grade for a task from the time it must run between aiding updates.

1. Why this matters

A gyro measures angular rate. To get an angle, the rate must be integrated, and integration accumulates every error: a tiny constant offset becomes a steadily growing heading error, and random noise becomes a random walk. The question "how long can my robot hold its heading without an external reference?" is answered entirely by the gyro's error model. This guide gives you the language datasheets use for that model, and the tool used to measure it: the Allan variance.

3. Background: random noise and random walks

3.1 Adding up random numbers

Take \(n\) independent random numbers, each with zero mean and variance \(\sigma^2\). Their sum also has zero mean, and because variances of independent quantities add, its variance is \(n\sigma^2\). So its standard deviation is \(\sigma\sqrt n\). The sum does not grow on average, but its spread grows as the square root of the number of terms.

Integrating a noisy gyro is exactly this. Sampling at \(f_s\) for time \(t\) sums \(n=f_st\) samples, each multiplied by \(\Delta t=1/f_s\). If the per-sample rate noise is \(\sigma_\omega\), the angle error after time \(t\) has standard deviation

$$\sigma_\theta(t)=\sigma_\omega\,\Delta t\,\sqrt{f_st}=\frac{\sigma_\omega}{\sqrt{f_s}}\sqrt t = N\sqrt t,\qquad N\equiv\frac{\sigma_\omega}{\sqrt{f_s}} .$$

\(N\) is the angle random walk coefficient. It is a property of the sensor, independent of the sample rate: sampling faster gives noisier individual samples, but more of them to average.

3.2 Noise density and sample rate

The same sensor sampled faster shows a larger per-sample standard deviation, \(\sigma_\omega=N\sqrt{f_s}\), because a higher sample rate admits a wider band of white noise. What stays the same is the noise density \(N\), and anything averaged over a fixed time.

Datasheet unit conversions (the most common source of simulation bugs).
  • ARW: \(N\,[^\circ/\sqrt{\text h}]\div60=N\,[^\circ/\sqrt{\text s}]\), which equals the noise density in (°/s)/√Hz.
  • Per-sample noise at rate \(f_s\): \(\sigma_\omega\,[^\circ/\text s]=N\,[^\circ/\sqrt{\text s}]\times\sqrt{f_s}\).
  • Bias: \(b\,[^\circ/\text h]\div3600=b\,[^\circ/\text s]\).
  • RRW: \(K\,[^\circ/\text h/\sqrt{\text h}]\div(3600\times60)=K\,[^\circ/\text s/\sqrt{\text s}]\).

4. The gyro error model and how each term grows

The measured rate about one axis is

$$\tilde\omega = (1+s)\,\omega + b_0 + b_{\mathrm{BI}}(t) + b_{\mathrm{RW}}(t) + n(t),$$

and the heading estimate is its integral, \(\hat\psi(t)=\int_0^t\tilde\omega\,d\tau\). Integrating each term separately gives its growth law:

TermDatasheet unitHeading errorRemoved by start-up calibration?
Turn-on bias \(b_0\)°/h or °/s\(b_0\,t\) (a ramp)Yes: average at rest and subtract
Scale factor \(s\)ppm\(s\times\)(angle turned)Needs a rate table
ARW \(N\)°/√h\(N\sqrt t\) (random)No; averages out only in the sense of §3
Bias instability \(B\)°/hroughly \(B\,t\) while correlatedNo: it changes after calibration
Rate random walk \(K\)°/h/√h\(K\,t^{3/2}/\sqrt3\)No

Bias instability deserves a note. The bias of a real gyro wanders slowly, with a spectrum that rises toward low frequencies (flicker, or 1/f, noise). It is typically modelled as a sum of Gauss–Markov processes. You can measure the bias at start-up, but within minutes it has moved. This is why estimators such as the Kalman filter of Guide 7 carry the gyro bias as a state and re-estimate it continuously.

Key idea. Calibration removes what is constant. Everything that changes during a run (bias instability, rate random walk, temperature effects) must be estimated online from aiding measurements, or tolerated.

5. Allan variance: measuring the noise

5.1 Why not just the standard deviation?

The standard deviation of a static recording mixes all noise types together and depends on the sample rate and the recording length. We need a statistic that separates short-term noise from long-term drift. The Allan variance does this by asking: how much does the average over a window of length \(\tau\) change from one window to the next?

5.2 The recipe

  1. Record the gyro at rest for a long time \(T\): hours for good sensors.
  2. Choose a cluster length \(\tau\). Split the record into consecutive clusters of length \(\tau\) and average each one: \(\bar\omega_1,\bar\omega_2,\dots\)
  3. Compute \(\sigma_A^2(\tau)=\tfrac12\big\langle(\bar\omega_{k+1}-\bar\omega_k)^2\big\rangle\), the mean over all adjacent pairs.
  4. Repeat for many \(\tau\), from one sample up to about \(T/10\), and plot the Allan deviation \(\sigma_A(\tau)\) on log–log axes.

The factor ½ makes \(\sigma_A^2\) equal the ordinary variance for white noise at \(\tau\) = one sample. A constant bias cancels in every difference, so the Allan variance is blind to constant bias; it measures only how the bias changes.

5.3 Reading the curve

Each noise type has a characteristic slope:

SlopeNoise typeHow to read the coefficient
−½White rate noise (ARW)\(N=\sigma_A(\tau=1\text{ s})\) on the −½ line
0Bias instabilityFlat floor \(=0.664\,B\), so \(B=\text{floor}/0.664\)
+½Rate random walk\(K=\sigma_A(\tau=3\text{ s})\) on the +½ line

Intuition for the shape: averaging longer suppresses white noise, so the curve falls. But the bias is slowly wandering, so beyond some \(\tau\) the average of one window no longer predicts the next, and the curve flattens and then rises. The bottom of the curve is the best bias stability you can achieve by averaging.

Practical cautions. Trust only \(\tau\lesssim T/10\): long clusters give few differences and a noisy estimate. Expect about ±20 % scatter on \(B\) from a single record of a few hours. Better sensors need longer recordings, because their floor appears at larger \(\tau\). Temperature changes during the recording masquerade as rate random walk.

Choose by asking two questions:

  1. How long must the system coast between aiding updates? Milliseconds for visual–inertial odometry at 20 Hz; seconds for a camera dropout; minutes for a GNSS outage in a tunnel.
  2. Which term dominates over that horizon, and is it acceptable? Read it from the growth laws or the Allan curve.

7. Worked examples

Example 5.1 — From datasheet to simulation

Problem. A datasheet gives ARW = 0.15 °/√h and bias instability 3 °/h. You simulate at 400 Hz. What per-sample noise standard deviation do you use, and what is the bias instability in °/s?

Solution.

  1. \(N=0.15/60=0.0025\) °/√s.
  2. \(\sigma_\omega=N\sqrt{f_s}=0.0025\times\sqrt{400}=0.0025\times20=0.05\) °/s.
  3. \(B=3/3600=8.3\times10^{-4}\) °/s.

Check. A common error is to use 0.15 °/s directly as the per-sample noise. That overstates the noise by a factor of 3 at 400 Hz, and the error changes with sample rate.

Example 5.2 — One minute of heading hold

Problem. Estimate the heading error after 60 s for (a) a consumer gyro with its turn-on bias uncompensated; (b) an industrial gyro, uncompensated; (c) the industrial gyro after start-up bias calibration.

Solution.

  1. (a) \(b_0=1\) °/s: error \(=1\times60=60^\circ\).
  2. (b) \(b_0=180\) °/h \(=0.05\) °/s: error \(=0.05\times60=3.0^\circ\).
  3. (c) Remaining terms: bias instability \(\approx B\,t=(3/3600)\times60=0.05^\circ\); ARW \(=N\sqrt t=0.0025\sqrt{60}=0.019^\circ\) (1σ).

Interpretation. Calibrating the turn-on bias reduces the error 60-fold for the industrial part. After that, bias instability and ARW are comparable over a minute.

Example 5.3 — Checking the √t law

Problem. A bias-free gyro has \(N=0.4\) °/√s. What is the 1σ angle spread at 4 s and at 16 s?

Solution. \(\sigma_\theta=N\sqrt t\): at 4 s, \(0.4\times2=0.8^\circ\); at 16 s, \(0.4\times4=1.6^\circ\).

Interpretation. Four times longer gives only twice the spread. That ratio (not a doubling of time giving double the error) is the signature of a random walk.

Example 5.4 — Reading an Allan plot

Problem. An Allan deviation plot shows the −½ line passing through \(2.5\times10^{-3}\) °/s at \(\tau=1\) s, and a flat floor at 2.0 °/h. Find \(N\) and \(B\) in datasheet units.

Solution.

  1. \(N=\sigma_A(1\text{ s})=2.5\times10^{-3}\) °/√s; ×60 gives \(N=0.15\) °/√h.
  2. \(B=\text{floor}/0.664=2.0/0.664=3.0\) °/h.

Answer. These are the industrial-grade values; the demo's "Allan deviation (industrial MEMS)" scenario reproduces them.

Example 5.5 — Scale-factor error

Problem. A gyro with a 1 % scale-factor error measures a 90° turn, then stays still for a minute. What is the heading error at the end?

Solution. The scale error applies only while turning: \(0.01\times90^\circ=0.9^\circ\). While stationary, \(\omega=0\) and the scale error contributes nothing more.

Interpretation. Scale-factor error depends on how much the robot turns, not on how long it runs. A robot that spins continuously (a lidar turret, a spinning drone) accumulates it quickly.

Example 5.6 — Choosing a grade for a camera dropout

Problem. A robot's visual odometry can drop out for up to 10 s. It has a consumer gyro. Heading must stay within 1°. Is the gyro adequate?

Solution.

  1. If the bias is uncompensated: \(1\ ^\circ/\text s\times10\ \text s=10^\circ\). Not adequate.
  2. If the filter has been estimating the bias while vision was available, the residual is about the bias instability: \((20/3600)\times10=0.056^\circ\).
  3. ARW: \((0.3/60)\sqrt{10}=0.016^\circ\).

Answer. Adequate, provided the gyro bias is estimated online. The requirement drives the filter design (a bias state) rather than a more expensive sensor.

8. Common mistakes

  • Unit errors between °/√h, °/√s and °/s, especially using the ARW figure as a per-sample standard deviation.
  • Treating one noisy run as a "drift" with a definite direction. ARW is zero-mean.
  • Expecting start-up calibration to remove bias instability.
  • Computing Allan variance from a record that is too short, or during warm-up.
  • Comparing sensors on "noise" without saying over what averaging time.

  1. A gyro has ARW 0.2 °/√h. What per-sample noise standard deviation should you simulate at 400 Hz? At 1 kHz?
  2. What is the 1σ heading error from ARW alone after 1 hour for the tactical grade (0.05 °/√h)?

12. Answers to practice problems

  1. \(N=0.2/60=0.00333\) °/√s. At 400 Hz: \(0.00333\times20=0.067\) °/s. At 1 kHz: \(0.00333\times31.6=0.105\) °/s.
  2. \(N\sqrt t\) with \(t=1\) h: 0.05° (1σ). Working in hours avoids the conversion.
  3. Each Allan term is a difference of adjacent averages, so a constant cancels. Allan variance tells you how fast the bias changes, i.e. how often you must re-estimate it; the constant part must be found by a separate rest average.
  4. The floor has not been reached, so \(B\) is below \(\sigma_A(200\,\text s)/0.664\). You can only give an upper bound; record for longer (the floor appears at larger τ for better sensors).
  5. 300 s. Consumer: BI \(20/3600\times300=1.67^\circ\), ARW \(0.3\sqrt{300/3600}=0.087^\circ\): total ≈ 1.8° — marginal. Industrial: \(3/3600\times300=0.25^\circ\) plus 0.043°. Industrial is the safe cheapest choice; consumer only just meets the requirement with no margin.
  6. \(N=\sigma_\omega/\sqrt{f_s}=0.03/10=0.003\) °/√s; ×60 gives 0.18 °/√h. (This assumes the per-sample spread is white noise; bias drift inflates it for long records.)
