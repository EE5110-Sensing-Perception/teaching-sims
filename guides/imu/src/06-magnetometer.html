By the end of this guide you should be able to:

  • describe the Earth's magnetic field by its intensity, inclination (dip) and declination;
  • compute magnetic and true heading from a level magnetometer reading;
  • derive tilt compensation, and explain why pitch and roll errors are amplified at high dip;
  • explain hard-iron and soft-iron distortion, and calibrate them with a compass swing and ellipse fit;
  • detect world-fixed magnetic disturbances with field-magnitude and dip checks.

1. Why this matters

Guides 4 and 5 left one gap: the accelerometer cannot see yaw, and the gyro's yaw drifts. A magnetometer measures the Earth's magnetic field, which has a horizontal component pointing (roughly) north, and so provides an absolute heading reference. It is cheap and small, and it is included in almost every "9-axis" IMU. It is also the least trustworthy sensor on the robot: steel, motors and wiring all distort the field. Using it well means knowing what can be calibrated and what can only be detected and rejected.

3. The Earth's field

Near the surface the Earth's field is roughly that of a bar magnet tilted about 10° from the rotation axis. At a given place it is a fixed vector, described in NED by

$$\mathbf b^n = F\begin{bmatrix}\cos I\cos D\\ \cos I\sin D\\ \sin I\end{bmatrix}.$$

Only the horizontal component \(H\) carries heading information, and it is the smaller part. At \(I=66^\circ\) (the demo's default, typical of Ireland and the UK), \(H\approx20\) µT and \(Z\approx45\) µT. Near the magnetic poles \(H\) vanishes and compasses become useless.

4. Heading from a level magnetometer

If the robot is level with heading \(\psi_m\) from magnetic north, the horizontal field seen in body axes (FRD) is

$$b_x=H\cos\psi_m,\qquad b_y=-H\sin\psi_m .$$

(When the nose turns east, magnetic north appears to the robot's left, so \(b_y\) goes negative.) Therefore

$$\psi_m=\operatorname{atan2}(-b_y,\ b_x),\qquad \psi=\psi_m+D .$$

Declination is not small everywhere: it is a few degrees in western Europe today but exceeds 10° in much of North America. A robot that fuses magnetometer heading with GNSS or a map in true north must apply it.

5. Tilt compensation

5.1 Why tilt matters

If the robot pitches, the body x axis tilts toward or away from the steep, strong vertical field, and part of \(Z\) leaks into \(b_x\). Because \(Z\) is about twice \(H\), a modest tilt produces a large heading error.

5.2 The compensation formula

Given roll \(\phi\) and pitch \(\theta\), usually from the accelerometer (Guide 4) or an attitude filter (Guide 7), undo them to get the field in a level frame that shares the robot's heading:

$$x_h = b_x\cos\theta + b_y\sin\phi\sin\theta + b_z\cos\phi\sin\theta,\qquad y_h = b_y\cos\phi - b_z\sin\phi,$$ $$\psi_m=\operatorname{atan2}(-y_h,\ x_h).$$

This is the first two rows of \(R_y(\theta)R_x(\phi)\,\mathbf b^b\): rotate back through roll, then pitch.

Error chain. Tilt compensation is only as good as the tilt. An error \(\delta\theta\) in pitch leaks \(Z\,\delta\theta\) into \(x_h\), giving a heading error of up to about \(\tan I\cdot\delta\theta\). At \(I=66^\circ\), \(\tan I=2.25\): every 1° of tilt error costs up to 2.25° of heading. Accelerometer tilt corrupted by acceleration (Guide 4 §3.3) therefore corrupts heading too.

6. Hard and soft iron

Material on the robot distorts the field the magnetometer sees:

$$\tilde{\mathbf b}=S\,\mathbf b+\mathbf h+\mathbf n .$$
  • Hard iron \(\mathbf h\): a permanent field that rotates with the robot. It adds a constant offset in body axes.
  • Soft iron \(S\): ferrous material that concentrates or bends the Earth's field. Its effect depends on the field's direction relative to the robot, which is a matrix acting on \(\mathbf b\).

Both are fixed to the robot, so both can be calibrated. If you rotate the robot through 360° while level, the true \((b_x,b_y)\) traces a circle of radius \(H\) centred on the origin. Hard iron shifts the circle; soft iron stretches and rotates it into an ellipse.

6.1 The compass swing and ellipse fit

  1. Rotate the robot slowly through a full turn while level, logging \((b_x,b_y)\).
  2. Fit a general conic \(ax^2+bxy+cy^2+dx+ey=1\) by linear least squares: each sample gives one equation that is linear in \((a,b,c,d,e)\).
  3. The centre of the conic is the hard-iron offset \(\mathbf c\). The quadratic part gives a matrix \(W\) that maps the ellipse onto a circle.
  4. Correct live data with \(\hat{\mathbf b}=W(\tilde{\mathbf b}-\mathbf c)\), then compute heading.

A level 2-D swing calibrates only the horizontal axes. A full 3-D calibration tumbles the sensor through as many orientations as possible and fits an ellipsoid; drones do this in the "compass calibration dance".

7. World-fixed disturbances: detect, don't calibrate

Steel beams, reinforced concrete, parked cars and power cables distort the field in the world. The distortion changes as the robot moves, so no calibration can remove it. Motor and actuator currents add time-varying fields on the robot. The defence is to detect bad samples:

$$\big|\,\|\tilde{\mathbf b}\|-F\,\big|<\epsilon_F\ (\text{e.g. }5\,\%),\qquad |\hat I-I|<\epsilon_I\ (\text{e.g. }3^\circ),$$

where \(\hat I\) is the dip computed from the measurement and the current attitude. If either check fails, skip the magnetometer update and let the gyro carry the heading (Guide 7). The checks are necessary, not sufficient: a disturbance that rotates the horizontal field without changing \(F\) or \(I\) passes them.

Robotics reality. Indoors and near vehicles, magnetometer heading is often unusable. Many robots instead get yaw from vision or LiDAR (visual–inertial or LiDAR–inertial odometry), which is why yaw is usually the weakest state in those systems.

8. Worked examples

Example 6.1 — Field components

Problem. The demo's default field has \(F=49.24\) µT and \(I=66.04^\circ\). Find \(H\) and \(Z\).

Solution. \(H=F\cos I=49.24\times0.406=20.0\) µT; \(Z=F\sin I=49.24\times0.914=45.0\) µT.

Interpretation. The heading signal is 20 µT; the vertical "nuisance" is 45 µT. A 1 µT disturbance is 5 % of \(H\), enough to swing heading by about 3°.

Example 6.2 — Level heading and declination

Problem. A level robot reads \(\mathbf b^b=[15,\ -10,\ 45]\) µT. Local declination is \(+12^\circ\) (east). Find the magnetic and true heading, and run the field check.

Solution.

  1. \(\psi_m=\operatorname{atan2}(-b_y,\ b_x)=\operatorname{atan2}(10,\ 15)=33.7^\circ\).
  2. \(\psi=\psi_m+D=33.7+12=45.7^\circ\).
  3. Check: \(\|\mathbf b\|=\sqrt{15^2+10^2+45^2}=48.5\) µT, within 1.5 % of 49.24. Dip \(=\arctan(45/\sqrt{15^2+10^2})=\arctan(45/18.0)=68.2^\circ\), 2.2° from 66.04°: passes a 3° threshold.

Answer. Magnetic heading 33.7°, true heading 45.7° (north-east).

Example 6.3 — Pitch without compensation

Problem. The robot faces magnetic east (\(\psi_m=90^\circ\)) and pitches 10° nose-up. What heading does a level-only formula report?

Solution.

  1. Level at east: \(\mathbf b^b=[0,\ -20.0,\ 45.0]\) µT.
  2. Pitching nose-up by \(\theta\) about body y mixes x and z: \(b_x=0\cdot\cos\theta - Z\sin\theta = -45.0\sin10^\circ=-7.81\) µT; \(b_y\) is unchanged at −20.0.
  3. \(\psi_m=\operatorname{atan2}(20.0,\ -7.81)=111.3^\circ\).

Answer. A 21.3° heading error from 10° of pitch. The demo shows the same value. With tilt compensation, \(x_h=b_x\cos\theta+b_z\sin\theta=-7.81(0.985)+44.3(0.174)=0.0\) and the heading returns to 90°.

Example 6.4 — Tilt error amplification

Problem. Tilt compensation uses a pitch estimate that is 1° wrong. The robot heads magnetic east. What heading error results?

Solution. The residual leak into \(x_h\) is about \(Z\,\delta\theta=45.0\times0.01745=0.785\) µT, compared with \(H=20\) µT across. The heading error is \(\arctan(0.785/20)\approx2.25^\circ\), i.e. \(\tan I\times1^\circ\).

Answer. About 2.25°, confirmed numerically. At north or south headings the same pitch error produces almost no heading error.

Example 6.5 — Hard iron from a swing

Problem. During a level swing, \(b_x\) ranges from −12 to +28 µT and \(b_y\) from −25 to +15 µT. The locus is a circle. Estimate the hard-iron offset.

Solution. For a circle, the centre is the midpoint of each range: \(c_x=(28+(-12))/2=8\) µT and \(c_y=(15+(-25))/2=-5\) µT. The radius, \((28-(-12))/2=20\) µT, equals \(H\), as it should.

Answer. \(\mathbf h\approx(8,\ -5)\) µT: the demo's "Hard-iron offset" scenario. The min/max method only works for circles; soft iron needs the ellipse fit.

Example 6.6 — Gating a disturbance

Problem. Driving past a parked car, the magnetometer magnitude reads 55.0 µT where the model predicts 49.24 µT. Should the sample be used?

Solution. Deviation \(=(55.0-49.24)/49.24=11.7\,\%\), above a 5 % threshold. Reject it and let the gyro carry heading until the checks pass again.

9. Common mistakes

  • Computing heading without tilt compensation on a robot that pitches or rolls.
  • Calibrating on the bench and then mounting the sensor near motors or batteries. Calibrate in the final installed configuration.
  • Swinging the robot while tilted during a 2-D calibration, which corrupts the fit.
  • Mixing magnetic heading with a map or GNSS in true north without adding declination.
  • Trusting the magnetometer indoors without gating.

13. Answers to practice problems

  1. \(\operatorname{atan2}(14.1,\ -14.1)=135^\circ\): south-east.
  2. Facing north, pitch rotates body x within the north–down plane. The field lies in that plane too, so \(b_x\) changes but \(b_y\) stays zero, and \(\operatorname{atan2}(0,b_x)=0\) as long as \(b_x\) stays positive.
  3. \(\tan20^\circ=0.36\) vs \(\tan66^\circ=2.25\): about 6× less sensitive near the magnetic equator.
  4. If tilted, the vertical field leaks into \(b_x,b_y\) by an amount that varies with heading, distorting the locus. The fit then attributes that distortion to soft iron and "corrects" the wrong thing.
  5. Fixable: hard iron, soft iron (both body-fixed). Not fixable: world-fixed distortion (steel structures, cars), time-varying fields from currents (unless modelled against current).
  6. The motor's field changes with current, so it is not a constant hard-iron offset. Fixes: move the magnetometer away (e.g. on a mast; the field falls roughly as \(1/r^3\)), or calibrate the field against measured motor current and subtract it.
