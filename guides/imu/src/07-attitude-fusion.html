By the end of this guide you should be able to:

  • explain low-pass and high-pass filtering, and the meaning of a filter time constant;
  • derive the complementary filter, and choose its time constant \(\tau\) and coefficient \(\alpha\) for a given loop rate;
  • state the Kalman filter's predict and update equations, and run one step by hand;
  • formulate the two-state [angle, gyro bias] Kalman filter, and relate its steady state to a complementary filter;
  • explain the Mahony filter's error vector and its gains \(K_p\) and \(K_i\);
  • decide which states are observable from a given set of sensors;
  • recognise when the "accelerometer measures gravity" assumption fails, and apply gating.

1. Why this matters

No single IMU sensor gives a good attitude. The gyro is smooth and responsive but drifts (Guide 5). The accelerometer knows where down is, on average, but is noisy and is fooled by acceleration (Guide 4). The magnetometer knows north, on average, but is fooled by iron (Guide 6). Sensor fusion combines them so that each covers the others' weaknesses. Every drone flight controller, legged robot and phone runs a filter like the ones in this guide, often thousands of times per second.

3. Background: filters as frequency splitters

3.1 A first-order low-pass filter

The simplest smoothing filter moves its output \(y\) toward its input \(u\) at a rate proportional to the gap:

$$\tau\,\dot y = u - y .$$

For a sudden step in \(u\) from 0 to 1, the output is \(y=1-e^{-t/\tau}\): 63 % of the way after \(\tau\), 95 % after \(3\tau\). Slow inputs pass through; fast wiggles are averaged away.

In the frequency domain (Laplace variable \(s\), with \(s=j2\pi f\) for sinusoids) this filter is \(\frac{1}{1+s\tau}\): gain 1 at low frequency, falling as \(1/f\) above \(f_c=1/(2\pi\tau)\). Its complement \(1-\frac{1}{1+s\tau}=\frac{s\tau}{1+s\tau}\) is a high-pass filter: gain 0 at DC, 1 at high frequency.

3.2 Discrete time

At sample interval \(\Delta t\), the same low-pass filter is \(y_k=\alpha y_{k-1}+(1-\alpha)u_k\), an exponential moving average. The relation between \(\alpha\) and \(\tau\) is

$$\tau=\frac{\alpha\,\Delta t}{1-\alpha},\qquad \alpha=\frac{\tau}{\tau+\Delta t}.$$
Important. \(\alpha\) alone means nothing: the same \(\alpha\) gives a different \(\tau\) at a different loop rate. Design with \(\tau\), then compute \(\alpha\) from your \(\Delta t\).

4. The complementary filter

4.1 The idea

Gyro angle \(\int\omega\,dt\)Accelerometer tilt
Fast motion (high frequency)goodnoisy; corrupted by acceleration
Slow / constant (low frequency)drifts with biasabsolute and unbiased

So: take the gyro's high frequencies and the accelerometer's low frequencies.

$$\hat\Theta = \underbrace{\frac{s\tau}{1+s\tau}}_{\text{high-pass}}\Theta_{\mathrm{gyro}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{1+s\tau}}_{\text{low-pass}}\Theta_{\mathrm{acc}} .$$

The two weights sum to one at every frequency, so if both sensors were perfect the output would equal the truth exactly: the filter introduces no lag or distortion of its own. That is what "complementary" means.

4.2 The one-line implementation

$$\hat\theta_k=\alpha\,(\hat\theta_{k-1}+\omega_k\Delta t)+(1-\alpha)\,\theta^{\mathrm{acc}}_k .$$

Read it as: predict with the gyro, then nudge a small fraction \(1-\alpha\) of the way toward the accelerometer. Rearranged for small \(\Delta t\), it becomes

$$\dot{\hat\theta}=\omega+\frac{1}{\tau}\big(\theta^{\mathrm{acc}}-\hat\theta\big):$$

gyro integration plus a proportional correction with gain \(1/\tau\). Keep this form in mind: the Mahony filter (§6) is its 3-D version.

4.3 Choosing \(\tau\)

  • Larger \(\tau\) (trust the gyro): smooth, rejects accelerometer noise and short accelerations, but a gyro bias \(b\) produces a steady error \(b\,\tau\) (see Example 7.3).
  • Smaller \(\tau\) (trust the accelerometer): little drift, but accelerometer noise and acceleration pass through.

Choose \(\tau\) longer than typical manoeuvres and noise correlation times, but short enough that \(b\,\tau\) is acceptable. Typical values are 0.5–5 s.

5. The Kalman filter

5.1 The idea: weigh by uncertainty

The complementary filter uses a fixed blend. A Kalman filter computes the blend from the uncertainties. It keeps a state estimate \(\hat{\mathbf x}\) and its covariance \(P\), and alternates two steps:

  1. Predict with a model (here, gyro integration). Uncertainty grows.
  2. Update with a measurement (here, accelerometer tilt). Uncertainty shrinks.

In one dimension, with Gaussian (normal) uncertainties, the update is a weighted average in which the weights are inverse variances:

$$K=\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^2}{\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^2+\sigma_{\mathrm{meas}}^2},\qquad \hat x=\hat x_{\mathrm{pred}}+K(z-\hat x_{\mathrm{pred}}),\qquad \sigma^2=(1-K)\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^2 .$$

5.2 The general equations

For a linear model \(\mathbf x_k=F\mathbf x_{k-1}+G\mathbf u_k+\mathbf w_k\) and measurement \(\mathbf z_k=H\mathbf x_k+\mathbf v_k\), with \(\mathbf w\sim\mathcal N(0,Q)\) and \(\mathbf v\sim\mathcal N(0,R)\):

StepEquations
Predict\(\hat{\mathbf x}^-=F\hat{\mathbf x}+G\mathbf u,\qquad P^-=FPF^\top+Q\)
Update\(K=P^-H^\top(HP^-H^\top+R)^{-1}\)
\(\hat{\mathbf x}=\hat{\mathbf x}^-+K(\mathbf z-H\hat{\mathbf x}^-)\)
\(P=(I-KH)P^-\)

5.3 The two-state attitude filter

Estimate the pitch and the gyro bias, \(\mathbf x=[\theta,\ b]^\top\). The gyro is the input:

$$\theta_k=\theta_{k-1}+(\tilde\omega_k-b_{k-1})\Delta t,\qquad b_k=b_{k-1}+w_b,$$ $$F=\begin{bmatrix}1&-\Delta t\\0&1\end{bmatrix},\quad G=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta t\\0\end{bmatrix},\quad H=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\end{bmatrix},\quad z_k=\theta^{\mathrm{acc}}_k .$$
  • \(Q\) holds the gyro noise (how much the angle prediction wanders per step) and the bias random walk (how fast the bias may change).
  • \(R\) is the variance of the accelerometer tilt.
  • The off-diagonal of \(P\) links angle and bias. When the accelerometer says the angle is higher than predicted, the filter infers the gyro under-reported, and lowers the bias estimate. That is how an angle measurement ends up estimating a rate bias.
Key idea. After a while \(P\) and \(K\) settle to constants. The angle update then is a complementary filter, with \(\alpha_{\mathrm{eq}}=1-K_\theta\). The Kalman filter adds two things: an online estimate of the gyro bias (which removes the \(b\tau\) error), and a principled way to tune the blend from sensor noise figures.
Caveat. The Kalman filter is optimal only if its model is right. It assumes the accelerometer error is white noise. A sustained acceleration violates that: the filter treats it as information, and may even learn a wrong bias.

6. The Mahony filter: attitude fusion in 3-D

In three dimensions the attitude is a rotation (Guide 1), so the "angle difference" of the complementary filter becomes a small rotation between two directions. The Mahony filter compares the direction of gravity predicted by the current attitude estimate with the direction measured by the accelerometer (and similarly for the magnetic field), and rotates the estimate to close the gap:

$$\dot{\hat q}=\tfrac12\,\hat q\otimes\big[0,\ \tilde{\boldsymbol\omega}-\hat{\mathbf b}+K_p\,\mathbf e\big],\qquad \dot{\hat{\mathbf b}}=-K_i\,\mathbf e,$$ $$\mathbf e=\hat{\mathbf a}\times\hat R^\top\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\-1\end{bmatrix}+\hat{\mathbf m}\times\hat R^\top\mathbf b_{\mathrm{ref}} .$$
  • \(\hat{\mathbf a}\) is the normalised accelerometer reading; \(\hat R^\top[0,0,-1]\) is where the estimate says "up" should be in body axes. Their cross product is perpendicular to both, with magnitude the sine of the angle between them: the axis and amount of rotation needed to align them.
  • \(K_p\) plays the role of \(1/\tau\): how fast to pull the estimate toward the measurement.
  • \(K_i\) integrates a persistent error into a gyro-bias estimate (integral action, as in a PI controller).

Mahony runs at kHz on a microcontroller and is the backbone of many drone flight controllers. Madgwick's filter makes the same correction with a gradient-descent step. An extended or error-state Kalman filter (EKF/ESKF) adds covariance and more states. All share the structure: predict with the gyro, correct with vector observations.

7. Observability

A state is observable if the measurements, over time, pin it down. Ask of every state in your filter: does any measurement change when this state changes?

  • Gravity is unchanged by rotation about the vertical. So an accelerometer corrects roll and pitch (and, through them, the horizontal gyro biases), but not yaw, and not the vertical gyro bias.
  • Without a heading reference, yaw error grows as \(\int b_z\,dt\) forever, and \(\hat b_z\) never converges.
  • Heading references: magnetometer (Guide 6), GNSS course over ground (when moving), visual landmarks, loop closure.

8. When gravity lies

Every accelerometer correction assumes \(\mathbf f\approx-\mathbf g\). During acceleration it is not, and the filter is pulled toward a wrong attitude (Guide 4 §3.3). Remedies, in increasing sophistication:

  1. Gating: skip the update when \(\big|\|\mathbf f\|-g\big|>\epsilon\). Cheap, but blind when the tilt and the acceleration happen to leave \(\|\mathbf f\|\approx g\).
  2. Adaptive \(R\): inflate the measurement noise when \(\big|\|\mathbf f\|-g\big|\) or the rotation rate is large, so the filter trusts the accelerometer less.
  3. Model the acceleration: estimate velocity (an INS with aiding, Guide 8) and subtract the true acceleration before using the accelerometer as a gravity sensor. This is what full navigation filters do.

9. Worked examples

Example 7.1 — From \(\alpha\) to \(\tau\), and back

Problem. Code uses \(\alpha=0.98\) in a 100 Hz loop. Find \(\tau\) and \(f_c\). The same code is moved, unchanged, to a 400 Hz loop: what are \(\tau\) and \(f_c\) now? What \(\alpha\) keeps \(\tau=0.5\) s at 400 Hz?

Solution.

  1. 100 Hz: \(\tau=0.98\times0.01/0.02=0.49\) s; \(f_c=1/(2\pi\times0.49)=0.325\) Hz.
  2. 400 Hz: \(\tau=0.98\times0.0025/0.02=0.1225\) s; \(f_c=1.30\) Hz. The filter now trusts the accelerometer four times more.
  3. \(\alpha=\tau/(\tau+\Delta t)=0.5/0.5025=0.99502\).

Example 7.2 — One complementary-filter step

Problem. \(\hat\theta_{k-1}=10.0^\circ\), gyro \(\omega_k=5\) °/s, \(\Delta t=0.01\) s, accelerometer tilt \(\theta^{\mathrm{acc}}_k=11.0^\circ\), \(\alpha=0.98\). Find \(\hat\theta_k\).

Solution.

  1. Gyro prediction: \(10.0+5\times0.01=10.05^\circ\).
  2. Blend: \(0.98\times10.05+0.02\times11.0=9.849+0.220=10.069^\circ\).

Interpretation. The accelerometer nudges the estimate by only 0.019°, 2 % of the 0.95° gap. Over many steps these nudges remove drift without letting single noisy samples through.

Example 7.3 — The complementary filter's bias error

Problem. The gyro has a bias \(b=0.8\) °/s and \(\tau=0.49\) s. The robot is stationary and level. Where does the complementary filter settle?

Solution. With error \(e=\hat\theta-\theta\), the continuous form gives \(\dot e=b-e/\tau\). At steady state \(\dot e=0\), so \(e=b\,\tau=0.8\times0.49=0.39^\circ\).

Interpretation. The demo confirms 0.392°. A complementary filter never fully removes a gyro bias: the error is proportional to \(\tau\). The Kalman filter (or Mahony's \(K_i\) term) estimates \(b\) and drives this error to zero.

Example 7.4 — A 1-D Kalman update

Problem. The gyro-based prediction of pitch is \(3^\circ\pm1.5^\circ\) (1σ). The accelerometer measures \(6^\circ\pm1^\circ\). Fuse them.

Solution.

  1. \(K=1.5^2/(1.5^2+1^2)=2.25/3.25=0.692\).
  2. \(\hat\theta=3+0.692\times(6-3)=5.08^\circ\).
  3. \(\sigma^2=(1-0.692)\times2.25=0.692\), so \(\sigma=0.83^\circ\).

Interpretation. The result is closer to the more certain measurement, and more certain (0.83°) than either input. This is Figure 4.

Example 7.5 — One step of the two-state filter

Problem. \(\Delta t=0.01\) s. Initial estimate \(\hat{\mathbf x}=[0^\circ,\ 0\ ^\circ/\text s]\), \(P=\operatorname{diag}(4,\ 1)\) (units deg², (deg/s)²), \(R=4\) deg² (2° accelerometer noise). Ignore \(Q\) for simplicity. The gyro reads 5 °/s and the accelerometer 1.0°. Run one predict and one update.

Solution.

  1. Predict state: \(\theta^-=0+(5-0)(0.01)=0.05^\circ\); \(b^-=0\).
  2. Predict covariance: \(P^-=FPF^\top=\begin{bmatrix}4.0001&-0.01\\-0.01&1\end{bmatrix}\). The \(-0.01\) is new: a larger bias would mean a smaller angle, so the two errors are now negatively correlated.
  3. Gain: \(K=P^-H^\top/(P^-_{11}+R)=[4.0001,\ -0.01]^\top/8.0001=[0.500,\ -0.00125]^\top\).
  4. Innovation: \(z-\theta^-=1.0-0.05=0.95^\circ\).
  5. Update: \(\theta=0.05+0.500\times0.95=0.525^\circ\); \(b=0-0.00125\times0.95=-0.0012\) °/s.
  6. Covariance: \(P=(I-KH)P^-\approx\begin{bmatrix}2.00&-0.005\\-0.005&1.00\end{bmatrix}\), so \(\sigma_\theta\) fell from 2° to 1.41°.

Interpretation. The angle moved halfway to the measurement because the two were equally uncertain. The bias moved only slightly, but in a sensible direction: the angle came out higher than the gyro predicted, so the gyro is probably reading low, and the bias estimate goes negative. Over hundreds of steps these small corrections converge on the true bias.

Example 7.6 — The Mahony error vector

Problem. The filter believes the robot is level, but it is actually rolled 5° right. Compute \(\mathbf e\) (accelerometer term only) and the initial correction rate with \(K_p=1\ \text s^{-1}\).

Solution.

  1. Measured, normalised (FRD): \(\hat{\mathbf a}=[0,\ -\sin5^\circ,\ -\cos5^\circ]=[0,\ -0.0872,\ -0.9962]\).
  2. Predicted "up" for a level estimate: \(\hat R^\top[0,0,-1]^\top=[0,0,-1]\).
  3. \(\mathbf e=\hat{\mathbf a}\times[0,0,-1]=[(-0.0872)(-1)-(-0.9962)(0),\ 0,\ 0]=[0.0872,\ 0,\ 0]\).
  4. Correction \(K_p\mathbf e=0.0872\) rad/s \(=5.0\) °/s about body +x, which increases the estimated roll toward the true 5°.

Interpretation. \(\|\mathbf e\|=\sin5^\circ\): the cross product measures the misalignment angle and its axis. With \(K_p=1\ \text s^{-1}\), the error decays with a time constant of about 1 s, like a complementary filter with \(\tau=1/K_p\).

Example 7.7 — Designing \(\tau\) for a surge

Problem. A robot accelerates at 2 m/s² for 4 s. Its gyro bias is 0.8 °/s. Compare \(\tau=0.49\) s and \(\tau=5\) s.

Solution. The accelerometer tilt error during the surge is \(\arctan(2/9.81)=11.5^\circ\). A complementary filter follows it as \(11.5(1-e^{-t/\tau})\):

  • \(\tau=0.49\) s: after 4 s, 11.5° (it follows the false tilt almost completely). Bias error \(b\tau=0.39^\circ\).
  • \(\tau=5\) s: after 4 s, 6.3°. But the steady bias error is \(0.8\times5=4.0^\circ\).

Interpretation. No single \(\tau\) wins: a longer \(\tau\) resists acceleration but amplifies bias error. The Kalman filter's bias state removes the bias error; gating (demo: about 40 % of samples rejected) or an acceleration model removes the surge error.

10. Common mistakes

  • Copying \(\alpha\) between systems with different loop rates.
  • Tuning a Kalman filter's \(Q\) and \(R\) by trial and error without relating them to the sensor datasheet (Guide 5).
  • Expecting a filter to estimate a state that no measurement can see (yaw, vertical gyro bias without a heading reference).
  • Using the accelerometer as a gravity sensor during sustained acceleration, braking or turns.
  • Integrating Euler angles in 3-D instead of a quaternion (Guide 1).

12. Practice problems

  1. You want \(\tau=2\) s in a 250 Hz loop. What \(\alpha\)?
  2. Which frequency band does each sensor contribute in a complementary filter, and why that way round?
  3. A 1-D prediction is \(10^\circ\pm2^\circ\) and a measurement \(12^\circ\pm2^\circ\). What is the fused estimate and its standard deviation?
  4. In the two-state filter, what happens to the bias estimate if \(Q\) for the bias is set to zero? If it is set very large?
  5. A quadrotor has an accelerometer and gyro but no magnetometer. Which of roll, pitch, yaw, \(b_x\), \(b_y\), \(b_z\) can its filter estimate while hovering?

  7. In the Mahony filter, what is the effect of setting \(K_i=0\)? Of setting \(K_p=0\)?

14. Answers to practice problems

  1. \(\Delta t=0.004\) s: \(\alpha=2/2.004=0.998\).
  2. Gyro: high frequencies (it is precise over short intervals but its integral drifts slowly). Accelerometer: low frequencies (unbiased on average, but noisy and disturbed instant to instant). The other way round would keep each sensor's worst feature.
  3. Equal variances: \(K=0.5\), estimate \(11^\circ\), \(\sigma=\sqrt{0.5\times4}=1.41^\circ\).
  4. \(Q_b=0\): the filter becomes certain of the bias over time and stops updating it, so it cannot follow bias instability. Very large \(Q_b\): the bias estimate chases every accelerometer disturbance and becomes noisy.
  5. Roll, pitch, \(b_x\), \(b_y\): yes (while hovering level). Yaw and \(b_z\): no, because gravity is invariant to rotation about the vertical.
  6. The accelerometer reads \(\arctan(5/9.81)=27^\circ\) nose-down during braking. Without gating the error follows \(27(1-e^{-t/1})\), reaching about 26° at 3 s, then decays with \(\tau=1\) s afterwards. With gating most samples are rejected and the error stays small, limited by gyro drift over 3 s.
  7. \(K_i=0\): no bias estimation, so a steady error \(b/K_p\) remains (like \(b\tau\) in the CF). \(K_p=0\): no correction at all; pure gyro integration, which drifts.
