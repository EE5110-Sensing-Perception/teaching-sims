By the end of this guide you should be able to:

  • explain dead reckoning and strapdown mechanisation, and write the navigation equations;
  • explain why an attitude error leaks gravity into the horizontal, and quantify it;
  • derive the \(t\), \(t^2\) and \(t^3\) error growth laws, and identify the dominant term on a log–log plot;
  • explain how zero-velocity updates and position fixes bound the error, and how each can fail;
  • list the states of an error-state Kalman filter and say which measurement makes each observable;
  • estimate how long an IMU of a given grade can navigate unaided.

1. Why this matters

An inertial navigation system (INS) computes position, velocity and attitude from an IMU alone: no satellites, no cameras, no radio. It works in tunnels, under water, indoors and under jamming, and it updates at hundreds of Hz. Its weakness is that every sensor error is integrated once or twice, so the position error grows without bound, often very quickly. Almost every modern localisation system (GNSS/INS, visual–inertial odometry, LiDAR–inertial odometry, legged-robot state estimation) is an INS plus an aiding sensor that keeps the drift in check. This guide explains the drift, and how aiding bounds it.

3. Background: integration amplifies errors

Integrating a constant \(c\) gives \(c\,t\); integrating again gives \(\tfrac12c\,t^2\); a third time gives \(\tfrac16c\,t^3\). Each integration raises the power of \(t\) by one. An INS integrates gyro rates once (to attitude) and accelerations twice (to position), and an attitude error feeds into the acceleration. So errors in different sensors reach position after one, two or three integrations.

4. Strapdown mechanisation

4.1 The equations

For robotics ranges and times we can treat the local navigation frame as flat and non-rotating. The mechanisation is then three coupled integrations:

$$\dot R^n_b=R^n_b[\boldsymbol\omega^b]_\times,\qquad \dot{\mathbf v}^n=R^n_b\,\mathbf f^b+\mathbf g^n,\qquad \dot{\mathbf p}^n=\mathbf v^n .$$
  1. Integrate the gyro to keep the attitude \(R^n_b\) (Guide 1).
  2. Rotate the accelerometer's specific force into the navigation frame: \(R^n_b\mathbf f^b\).
  3. Add gravity back. The accelerometer measures \(\mathbf f=\mathbf a-\mathbf g\) (Guide 4), so \(\mathbf a=\mathbf f+\mathbf g\).
  4. Integrate to velocity, then to position.

4.2 Gravity leakage

Step 3 cancels gravity only if the attitude is exactly right. If the estimated attitude is tilted by a small angle \(\delta\theta\), the rotated specific force (which is about \(g\) upward) acquires a horizontal component \(g\sin\delta\theta\approx g\,\delta\theta\) that is not cancelled. The INS believes the vehicle is accelerating horizontally.

Key idea. \(0.1^\circ\) of tilt error \(=1.745\times10^{-3}\) rad, which leaks \(9.81\times1.745\times10^{-3}=0.0171\) m/s² \(=1.7\) mg. Attitude accuracy and accelerometer bias are two sides of the same coin: a 0.1° alignment error costs as much as a 1.7 mg accelerometer bias.

4.3 What the flat-Earth model leaves out

Full navigation-grade mechanisation adds the Earth's rotation (15 °/h), transport rate (the navigation frame rotating as you travel over a curved Earth), Coriolis terms, and a gravity model that varies with latitude and height. It also has an unstable vertical channel: height errors change the computed gravity in a way that makes them grow exponentially, so height is always aided by a barometer or GNSS. Over hours, the horizontal tilt errors are bounded by Schuler feedback into an oscillation with period \(2\pi\sqrt{R_E/g}=84.4\) min. None of this matters over the seconds-to-minutes horizons of most robots, but it dominates for aircraft and ships.

5. Unaided error growth

5.1 Deriving the growth laws

Follow each error source through the integrations:

  • Initial velocity error \(\delta v\): integrated once, \(\delta p=\delta v\,t\).
  • Accelerometer bias \(b_a\): \(\delta v=b_at\), \(\delta p=\tfrac12b_at^2\).
  • Initial tilt \(\delta\theta\): leaks \(g\,\delta\theta\), which acts like an accelerometer bias: \(\delta p=\tfrac12g\,\delta\theta\,t^2\).
  • Pitch/roll gyro bias \(b_g\): the tilt error grows, \(\delta\theta=b_gt\); the leaked acceleration grows, \(g\,b_gt\); integrating twice more, \(\delta p=\tfrac16g\,b_g\,t^3\).
  • Yaw gyro bias \(\varepsilon\) while moving at speed \(v\): the heading error \(\varepsilon t\) turns forward velocity into a cross-track velocity \(v\,\varepsilon t\), so \(\delta p=\tfrac12v\,\varepsilon\,t^2\) sideways.
SourcePosition errorLog–log slope
Initial velocity error \(\delta v\)\(\delta v\,t\)1
Accelerometer bias \(b_a\)\(\tfrac12b_at^2\)2
Initial tilt \(\delta\theta\)\(\tfrac12g\,\delta\theta\,t^2\)2
Yaw gyro bias \(\varepsilon\) at speed \(v\)\(\tfrac12v\,\varepsilon\,t^2\) (cross-track)2
Pitch/roll gyro bias \(b_g\)\(\tfrac16g\,b_g\,t^3\)3

On a log–log plot each term is a straight line with slope equal to its power of \(t\). The largest term at a given time dominates, and because the slopes differ, the dominant term changes with the time horizon: short coasts are dominated by velocity and accelerometer errors, long coasts by gyro bias.

6. Aiding bounds the error

6.1 Zero-velocity updates

If the vehicle is stationary, its true velocity is exactly zero. Feeding "\(\mathbf v=0\)" to a Kalman filter as a measurement resets the velocity error, and, through the filter's correlations, corrects tilt and accelerometer bias too. Foot-mounted pedestrian navigation applies a ZUPT at every footstep, when the shoe is flat on the ground, and achieves errors under 1 % of distance travelled with consumer IMUs.

The detector is the weak link. A ZUPT detector must decide from the IMU when the vehicle is stationary, typically by thresholding the gyro rate and the deviation of \(\|\mathbf f\|\) from \(g\). But a vehicle cruising at constant velocity produces the same IMU readings as a stationary one: no rotation and \(\|\mathbf f\|=g\). A false ZUPT during cruise tells the filter the velocity is zero when it is not, which is far worse than no ZUPT. Vehicles confirm stops with wheel speed, vision or a known context (e.g. a foot's stance phase).

6.2 Position and velocity fixes

GNSS, wheel odometry, visual odometry and LiDAR scan matching provide position or velocity measurements at a few Hz to tens of Hz. Between fixes the INS drifts; at each fix a Kalman filter pulls the solution back. The error becomes a sawtooth: its peak is set by the drift over one fix interval, not over the whole mission.

6.3 The error-state Kalman filter

The demo's filter only estimates position and velocity, so the heading error is never corrected and the sawtooth peaks still creep upward. A full GNSS/INS or VIO filter is an error-state Kalman filter (ESKF). The INS runs the nonlinear mechanisation at full rate; the filter estimates the small errors in it, and feeds them back:

$$\delta\mathbf x=\big[\delta\mathbf p,\ \delta\mathbf v,\ \delta\boldsymbol\theta,\ \mathbf b_a,\ \mathbf b_g\big]\quad(15\text{ states}).$$
StateMade observable by
\(\delta\mathbf p\)position fixes (GNSS, map matching, loop closure)
\(\delta\mathbf v\)velocity fixes, ZUPT, or position fixes over time
Roll and pitch errorsvelocity/position measurements (through gravity leakage), or the accelerometer at rest
Yaw errora heading reference, or horizontal acceleration while aided (turns and speed changes make yaw error visible)
\(\mathbf b_a\)aiding over time; the horizontal components are hard to separate from tilt until the vehicle manoeuvres
\(\mathbf b_g\)aiding over time, via the attitude errors they cause

The error-state form is preferred because the errors are small (so linearisation is accurate), the attitude error can be a 3-vector without singularities (Guide 1 §5.3), and the filter can run slower than the IMU.

7. How long can you coast?

To budget a coast:

  1. Take the residual errors after calibration and online estimation: typically the bias instability of the gyro and accelerometer, plus ARW and VRW (Guide 5). If the turn-on bias is not calibrated or estimated, use that instead.
  2. Evaluate each growth law at the coast time and sum (or take the largest).
  3. The time at which the total reaches your tolerance is the coast time. Its inverse sets the minimum aiding rate.

Rule of thumb for robotics: consumer MEMS is fine when aiding arrives at 1–10 Hz. Unaided coasting beyond a second or two needs a better grade, or motion constraints such as ZUPT, or non-holonomic constraints (a wheeled vehicle cannot move sideways).

8. Worked examples

Example 8.1 — Tilt equals bias

Problem. An INS starts with a 0.1° pitch alignment error. Find the equivalent accelerometer bias and the position error after 60 s.

Solution.

  1. \(\delta\theta=0.1^\circ=1.745\times10^{-3}\) rad.
  2. Leaked acceleration: \(g\,\delta\theta=9.807\times1.745\times10^{-3}=0.0171\) m/s² \(=1.75\) mg.
  3. \(\delta p=\tfrac12(0.0171)(60^2)=30.8\) m.

Answer. 30.8 m after one minute; the demo's "0.1 deg tilt = 1.7 mg bias" scenario gives 30.8 m.

Example 8.2 — The cubic term

Problem. A pitch gyro has a residual bias of 0.01 °/s. Find the position error at 30 s and at 60 s.

Solution.

  1. \(b_g=0.01^\circ/\text s=1.745\times10^{-4}\) rad/s.
  2. \(\delta p(30)=\tfrac16(9.807)(1.745\times10^{-4})(30^3)=\tfrac16(1.711\times10^{-3})(27\,000)=7.7\) m.
  3. \(\delta p(60)=\tfrac16(1.711\times10^{-3})(216\,000)=61.6\) m.

Interpretation. Doubling the time multiplies the error by \(2^3=8\). The demo's "Pitch gyro bias: t^3" scenario reaches 61.6 m at 60 s.

Example 8.3 — Yaw bias on a straight road

Problem. A robot drives straight at 5 m/s with an uncorrected yaw-gyro bias of 0.2 °/s. How far off track is it after 20 s?

Solution. \(\varepsilon=0.2^\circ/\text s=3.49\times10^{-3}\) rad/s. Cross-track error \(\tfrac12v\varepsilon t^2=\tfrac12(5)(3.49\times10^{-3})(400)=3.5\) m.

Interpretation. The heading error is then 4°. The small-angle formula holds while \(\varepsilon t\) stays small; on a circle (the demo's "Yaw gyro bias on a circle") the error shows up as a spiral.

Example 8.4 — Coast budget for three grades

Problem. A calibrated IMU coasts for 60 s on a straight path at 5 m/s. The residuals are the bias instabilities: accelerometer 100 / 20 / 10 µg and gyro 20 / 3 / 0.5 °/h for consumer / industrial / tactical. Which term dominates, and what total should you expect?

Solution. Consumer:

  1. Accel: \(b_a=100\times10^{-6}\times9.807=9.8\times10^{-4}\) m/s², so \(\tfrac12b_at^2=1.8\) m.
  2. Pitch gyro: \(b_g=20/3600=5.56\times10^{-3}\) °/s \(=9.7\times10^{-5}\) rad/s, so \(\tfrac16g\,b_gt^3=34\) m.
  3. Yaw gyro: \(\tfrac12v\,\varepsilon\,t^2=\tfrac12(5)(9.7\times10^{-5})(3600)=0.9\) m.

The \(t^3\) gyro term dominates: about 34 m (the demo shows 31 m, as the terms partly cancel). Repeating for industrial gives about 5 m, and for tactical under 1 m (demo: 4.5 m and 0.55 m).

Interpretation. For long coasts, the gyro, not the accelerometer, sets the error, because its error is integrated three times.

Example 8.5 — Fixes every second

Problem. GNSS arrives at 1 Hz and the INS velocity is well corrected at each fix. The consumer accelerometer is not calibrated (turn-on bias 40 mg). Estimate the peak position error between fixes. What if fixes drop out for 10 s?

Solution.

  1. \(b_a=40\times10^{-3}\times9.807=0.392\) m/s².
  2. Over 1 s: \(\tfrac12(0.392)(1^2)=0.20\) m, small compared with GNSS noise.
  3. Over 10 s: \(\tfrac12(0.392)(100)=19.6\) m.

Interpretation. Frequent aiding hides a poor IMU. An outage exposes it, which is exactly why the filter should estimate \(\mathbf b_a\) while fixes are available: after estimation the residual (100 µg) gives only 0.05 m over the same 10 s.

Example 8.6 — The Schuler period

Problem. Compute the Schuler period with Earth radius \(R_E=6371\) km.

Solution. \(T=2\pi\sqrt{R_E/g}=2\pi\sqrt{6.371\times10^6/9.807}=2\pi\times806=5064\) s \(=84.4\) min.

Interpretation. This is the period of a pendulum as long as the Earth's radius. Over a few minutes it has no effect, which is why the robotics growth laws are polynomial.

9. Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to add gravity back after rotating the specific force, or adding it with the wrong sign for the frame (NED \(+g\) down, ENU \(-g\) along up).
  • Budgeting a coast with the accelerometer bias only, and missing the \(t^3\) gyro term.
  • Trusting an IMU-only stationarity detector on a wheeled or flying vehicle.
  • Estimating position and velocity but not attitude and biases, so the aiding hides, rather than fixes, the underlying errors.
  • Assuming a "calibrated" IMU has zero bias. Calibration removes the turn-on bias; bias instability remains.

13. Answers to practice problems

  1. \(0.1\times60=6\) m, growing only linearly. Example 8.1's tilt gives 30.8 m because it grows quadratically.
  2. \(\delta\theta=b_a/g=5\times10^{-3}\) rad \(=0.29^\circ\).
  3. Accelerometer bias and tilt error (leaked gravity) both look like acceleration; gyro bias makes the tilt error grow. A ZUPT (velocity = 0) stops it.
  4. A pitch or roll error leaks gravity (9.81 m/s², always present) into the horizontal, adding two more integrations. A yaw error only rotates the existing horizontal velocity, which is zero when stationary and \(v\) when moving, so it gives \(t^2\) growth proportional to speed.
  5. At \(t=5\) s: gyro \(t^3\) term \(\tfrac16(9.807)(9.7\times10^{-5})(125)=0.02\) m; accelerometer \(\tfrac12(9.8\times10^{-4})(25)=0.012\) m. Both are centimetres, below GNSS noise. The velocity error left after each fix usually dominates instead (e.g. 0.1 m/s × 5 s = 0.5 m).
  6. Odometry gives forward velocity (and zero lateral/vertical velocity via non-holonomic constraints), making \(\delta\mathbf v\), roll/pitch, \(\mathbf b_a\) and the horizontal \(\mathbf b_g\) observable. Absolute position and yaw drift slowly (yaw only through turning geometry); they need a map, landmarks, a magnetometer or GNSS.
  7. If the tilt is wrong, leaked gravity makes the INS velocity grow while stationary. The ZUPT observes that velocity as an error; the filter's covariance links velocity error to tilt error, so part of the correction goes into the attitude.
